## 1. Principles from the book

### Phase 1: Dream buyer and the market

S1. **Define a dream buyer before any message or ad.** Specificity beats breadth. "Hangs out on Facebook" is too general. Name the exact places (groups, sites, habits). Where the buyer hangs out and gets information determines where to advertise, the tone, and the vocabulary.

S2. **Nine questions to define the dream buyer:** (1) where they congregate, online and offline; (2) where they get information when researching; (3) biggest frustrations and challenges; (4) hopes, dreams, desires; (5) biggest fears; (6) preferred form of communication; (7) exact phrases and vernacular they use; (8) what a day in their life looks like; (9) what makes them happy. Write answers as a plain sentence, e.g. "When [buyer] is curious about a topic, the first place they go is ___."

S3. **Use the buyer's own language.** Quoting Robert Collier: "Enter the conversation already taking place in the customer's mind." Copy should use the words the buyer uses in their own frustrations.

S4. **Focus is a feature.** Targeting a dream buyer does not exclude other buyers. It concentrates budget and message on the most likely buyer.

S5. **Educate before you sell.** The book's core claim: prospects who are uninformed are in a state of uncertainty, and people don't buy in that state. Educating them makes them likelier to buy and makes them likelier to buy from the educator. The aim is that prospects ask to speak to you rather than you chasing them.

S6. **Attract, educate, nurture, get action.** A system takes cold traffic and warms it, so the seller talks only to interested prospects. The book's figure is that most prospects (it cites 97%) are not ready to buy now. The system works on that large group.

S7. **Prospect pyramid.** Roughly 3% are ready to buy now. The rest are at earlier stages. The book's approach is to move people up the pyramid with content rather than pitch them directly.

### Phase 2: High-Value Content Offer (HVCO) and value-based marketing

S8. **HVCO = free, substantive value in exchange for name and email.** Formats: reports, ebooks, videos, cheat sheets. It must deliver on its promise. The book rejects "cheap gibberish" and two-page promotional reports.

S9. **Give before asking.** Value-based marketing creates goodwill and positions the seller as the expert. The book's rule: "The temperature of your marketing message must match the temperature of your traffic." Cold traffic gets education, not a hard sell.

S10. **The first exchange of value sets the expectation for paid work.** The book's goal is that the free piece makes the prospect think: "If this is what they give away for free, imagine what their paid products are like."

### Phase 3: Opt-in page

S11. **Opt-in page has one purpose: get contact details for the HVCO.** The book criticizes sending cold traffic straight to a homepage or a sales page. Opt-in elements include a headline, a subheadline that restates the offer and what the reader gets, short "fascination" bullets, and a visual of the content. Use the Phase 1 pain and desire language in the copy.

### Phase 4: Godfather Offer

S12. **Sell what the market wants, not what you want to build.** Start from market research (the Halo research in Phase 1), then build the offer. The book says to write the sales message before building the product, make the biggest honest claim, and then build what delivers it. Starving market first, product second.

S13. **Do not dress up an offer the market doesn't want.** "Good enough won't cut it." If research shows the offer is not what the market is starving for, pivot. Do not argue yourself into it.

S14. **Detail Sheet: features to benefits.** Two columns. List every feature. Convert each feature into a benefit. The book says this step is where offers "almost write themselves" and that most copywriters rush it.

S15. **Offer basics.** Define: what you sell, how much it costs, who acts immediately, and how they claim it. Draft the most powerful offer in a few lines, ignoring rules at first ("make it outrageous"), then pull it back to something you can actually deliver and that the regulations allow.

### Phase 5 and Phase 6: Traffic and Magic Lantern

S16. **Magic Lantern sequence.** Traffic source (ads or search) sends people to an opt-in page. They get the HVCO, then a thank-you page presents the Godfather Offer. A follow-up sequence of two or three value-only videos or emails moves the undecided majority forward. Each piece ends with a soft call to action to book a time, not a hard sell.

S17. **The undecided majority is the bigger opportunity.** The book says the 97% who are not buying now are the larger profit pool, and value-only nurture turns part of them into buyers. This is the "gravy" on top of the roughly 3% who buy immediately.

S18. **Traffic cost benchmark from the book:** roughly $2 per click or less, with its opt-in logic. Treat this as dated and channel-specific. Verify against our own tests before using it as a planning figure.

### Phase 7: Sales conversion

S19. **Sell by diagnosis first.** "A prescription without a diagnosis is malpractice." Ask where the pain is before presenting. Qualify the prospect and decline the poor fits: "if what you're selling doesn't deliver your prospect to their ultimate desired outcome, don't sell to them."

S20. **Free, zero-pressure sales conversation** of 30, 45, or 60 minutes is the book's recommended close for services. The book claims it can close 80% or more, depending on results. This is the author's claim and should not be used as a planning number.

### Phase 8: Email (opening sections)

S21. **Deliverability before design.** Plain, personal-looking emails get delivered. Heavy images, logo headers, and promotional buttons read as commercial mail and reduce inbox placement. The book's argument: undelivered mail is never opened, and unopened mail sells nothing.

S22. **Authentication and sender reputation.** The book says serious email needs proper authentication, a good sender score, a clean sending IP, and a good sender domain and reputation.

S23. **Warm new sending infrastructure slowly.** Start with low volume, then increase.

S24. **Rotate senders.** Sending from several named people improves open rates, which compounds into sender reputation.

S25. **Content first, offer second.** Emails should stand alone as valuable content. Offers can go in a P.S. The book calls this optional but not a hard rule.

## 3. Measurement

Track these per listing and per channel before scaling anything:

- Upwork: proposals sent, reply rate, interview rate, hire rate, average contract value, per tier. Log in `UPWORK/RESEARCH_TRACKER.md` or a new metrics file.
- LinkedIn: impressions and comments on the education posts, DMs started from content, calls booked, calls to close.
- Diagnosis: for each call, record whether the diagnostic questions were asked before the price was given, and whether the prospect was declined as a poor fit.
- Owned list (when it exists): opt-in rate from the HVCO and the Phase 8 deliverability checks (inbox placement, bounce rate).

## 4. Scope limits and things the book does not settle for us

- The book's sales numbers (80% close, $2 per click, 3% buy-now) are author claims from an earlier market. Do not use them as plans.
- Upwork and LinkedIn are rented channels with their own rules. Off-platform opt-in and contact-info pushing are barred on Upwork. LinkedIn automation and volume are capped by the limits file. The book's funnel does not transfer as written.
- The book assumes we own an email list and landing page. We do not, yet.
- The Phase 2 step the book says to take "immediately before creating a HVCO, setting up a website, landing page or running ads" was in the text but not captured in full in this pass. Re-read that passage before designing an HVCO.
