## OPTION C — "Two things this leaves out" (RECOMMENDED)
~920 chars, balanced, engineer-to-seller tone.

Two things this leaves out:

1. Training. You can't compare per-query inference cost and call it a day. The energy cost of training frontier models is massive, and we're running more of those runs every quarter.

2. Jevons paradox. When something gets 10x cheaper and faster, people don't do the same work with less energy — they do 10x more work with the same energy (often more). Efficiency gains don't always reduce total consumption; they often increase it.

That said — the core point that moving repetitive manual work off humans is a win, I buy that. Nobody's arguing against automating "move file from A to B."

It's the "so stop asking about energy" leap I don't buy. Energy IS a real constraint. Water use at data centers IS a real constraint. Asking about those things isn't anti-AI, it's being an engineer.

What's the most energy-intensive thing you've seen teams ship that didn't actually need AI at all?

## OPTION B — Shorter / punchier (pushback on the human-as-compute framing)
~695 chars, sharper, slightly more confrontational.

Honest take: the "AI uses less energy than a human" argument works until you remember that humans do a lot more than write status updates, and AI doesn't just replace one human answering one query.

Per-query inference is cheap. Training runs are not. And when efficiency goes up, total usage explodes — that's Jevons paradox, not some hypothetical.

Also worth saying: framing a human being as "20 years of three meals a day just to write a status update" is a pretty weird way to talk about people. We're not compute units.

Real question for builders: are we optimizing for less energy per query, or are we just using efficiency as a shield to ask fewer questions about what we build and why?

**Why you'd pick this:** if you want to push back harder on the "humans as inefficient compute" framing. The "we're not compute units" line is strong and memorable. Riskier because it calls out her specific line directly — which might land well or might annoy her.
