## 📅 DAY 2 — Tue Aug 25
**Pillar:** AI Ethics (ETHICS)
**Style:** Opinion Piece (OPENION)
**Hashtags:** #SoftwareEngineering #AIEthics #BuildResponsibly

"Move fast and break things" was bad advice for social media.

It's dangerous advice for AI.

Broken UI gets fixed with a hotfix. A biased computer-vision model trained on Western faces misclassifying darker-skinned users? That doesn't just "break." It harms.

I say this as someone who builds AI and computer-vision systems for a living — agricultural AI, production CV pipelines, LLM-powered features. I'm not on the sidelines. I ship.

Here's the thing: moving fast works when the cost of being wrong is a missed metric or a refund. When the cost is someone's loan application, medical diagnosis, or access to essential services? Slow is smooth and smooth is fast.

Three questions I run before any AI feature leaves my machine:
→ Who could this get wrong, and what happens to them when it does?
→ Can I explain why it made the decision it just made?
→ If I'm wrong about how well it works, how will I know — and how fast can I fix it?

If any answer is "I don't know," it doesn't ship.

That's not being anti-AI. That's being an engineer.

Real talk — what's the most reckless AI "move fast" thing you've seen someone ship? 👇

## 📅 DAY 3 — Wed Aug 26
**Pillar:** Software Engineering (SE)
**Style:** Educational Framework (FRAME)
**Hashtags:** #SoftwareEngineering #CodeReview #DeveloperTips

I review a lot of pull requests.

Most of them are fine. Some are great. And the ones that need work usually need the same things fixed — not because the engineers are bad, but because nobody teaches you how to write a PR that makes the reviewer's job easy.

Here's the 4-part checklist I started running on every PR before I open it.

→ Does the title explain WHAT changed and WHY, not just the ticket number? "Fix checkout crash on slow networks" beats "JIRA-1234" every single time.
→ Is the diff scoped to one problem? If I'm fixing auth and refactoring the database layer and updating the README in one PR, nobody can review that cleanly. Split it.
→ Did I leave comments on the weird parts? If I had to write a hack because of a third-party quirk or a legacy constraint, I say so in the code. Future me — and the reviewer — will thank me.
→ Did I test the thing the user actually does? Not just the function. The whole flow. Click the button. Fill the form. Submit. Watch it break. Fix it.

Takes me 10 extra minutes. Saves hours of back-and-forth.

Code review isn't a gate. It's a conversation. Make it easy for people to help you.

I'm deep in a computer-vision project this week that has nothing to do with chatbots, generative AI, or anything trendy.

I'm looking at sorghum stalks.

For context — part of my research work involves CV models that evaluate lodging resistance in crops. Lodging is when a plant falls over before harvest. It destroys yield, and it's a real problem for smallholder farmers across West Africa who don't have the resources big farms do.

The interesting part? Almost nothing in my day job looks like the computer vision tutorials online.

→ Tutorials show you perfect datasets. Real crop images have motion blur, uneven sunlight, dust on the lens, plants half-obscured by other plants.
→ Tutorials give you 98% accuracy on MNIST and call it a day. Real agricultural CV needs to work in a field at 2 PM in 38-degree heat with spotty internet.
→ Tutorials make it look like the model is the hard part. The model is 30% of the work. The hard part is data collection, edge cases, deployment on devices farmers actually own, and making sure the thing keeps working after I leave.

That last part is the difference between a research demo and a system people use.

Nobody posts about that.

I work remotely from Lagos for clients in North America and Europe.

The longer answer is what I want to talk about.

There's a specific kind of engineer you become building from a place where things don't always work. You learn to write code that degrades gracefully because the network will drop mid-request. You don't learn those things in a Silicon Valley co-working space with fiber internet and redundant power. You learn them when your generator goes off mid-deploy and your app has to handle it without taking down production.

The "disadvantages" of building from Lagos have made me a better engineer. Not in spite of them. Because of them.

## 📅 DAY 6 — Sat Aug 29
**Pillar:** Learning Meta-Skills (LEARN)
**Style:** Framework (FRAME)
**Hashtags:** #LearningToCode #SoftwareEngineering #TutorialHell

If you're trying to learn a new technology right now — new language, new framework, new domain — here's what I do.

It's not a roadmap. It's not a bootcamp. It's four steps I've used to pick up React, Go, PyTorch, OpenCV, Solidity, and Shopify development in the last 3 years. Some of them went deeper than others. The process is the same.

1. **Spend one day with the docs.** Not a tutorial. The actual documentation. Build the smallest thing the docs walk you through. Don't skip this — tutorials lie, docs don't.
2. **Build one stupid thing without following anything.** Not a to-do app. Something you actually want. A script that renames files. A dashboard for your personal budget. Something dumb and yours.
3. **Break it on purpose.** Remove the error handling. Send bad input. See what fails. Then fix it. You don't know a tool until you've watched it scream.
4. **Ship it to somewhere real.** Not local. Deploy it. Send the link to one friend. Put it on GitHub. It doesn't have to be good. It has to exist outside your machine.

That entire loop takes about a week per technology. It won't make you an expert. It'll make you unafraid.

Expertise comes after that — but fear is what stops most people before they even start.

I want to talk about one thing that cost me early in my career — and I don't see it discussed enough.

Cutting corners when nobody's watching.

Early on I was working on a feature for a client. The deadline was tight. I could take the extra day to write proper tests, handle the edge cases, document the weird parts. Or I could ship something that looked done, pocket the payment, and hope nothing broke.

I shipped. Things broke. Not catastrophically — but badly enough that the client noticed. Badly enough that I lost trust I spent months earning.

Years later, what I learned from that isn't "write more tests." That's obvious.

What I learned is this: the code you write when nobody is auditing you — that's the code that defines what kind of engineer you are.

Not the clever algorithm. Not the clever refactor. The boring stuff. The error handling. The comment you almost didn't write. The 20 minutes you spend cleaning up before you open the PR. The client will never know you did it. But you will.

My faith shapes that for me. Not because I'm perfect at it — because I'm not, and I've been on the wrong side of it enough times to know. Character isn't something you display. It's something you practice when it's inconvenient.

Do it right when nobody's watching. That's the whole thing.

**Pillar:** Software Engineering (SE)
**Style:** Personal Story (STORY)
**Hashtags:** #Debugging #SoftwareEngineering #LessonsLearned

The bug that taught me the most wasn't a clever race condition or a subtle memory leak.

It was a typo.

A few months ago I was debugging a production issue. Emails weren't sending from a web app I built. Not all of them — just the ones triggered after a specific user action. Checked the mail service. Checked the queue. Checked the async worker. Added logging everywhere. Spent two evenings on it.

The bug was one lowercase L vs uppercase i in an environment variable name.

Two hours of digging for a typo I made at 2 AM three weeks earlier.

Here's why that bug mattered more than most of the "impressive" bugs I've fixed: it taught me that production doesn't care how clever your code is. It breaks on the boring stuff. The missed character. The wrong env var. The truthy check that should've been a strict equals. The off-by-one.

Senior engineers aren't the ones who write flawless code. They're the ones who check the boring stuff first before reaching for the clever explanation.

When something "was working yesterday," I start with three things:
→ Did anything change in environment or config?
→ Is the thing I'm debugging actually where I think it is?
→ Did I typo something embarrassing?

Saves me hours. Every single week.

A client asked me to "add AI" to their app last week.

Here's the thing: AI isn't a seasoning you sprinkle on a broken product to make it feel smarter. If your core app is broken, a chatbot won't fix it. It'll just cost you more money.

I've shipped AI features users loved. I've also talked clients out of AI they didn't need. Both are engineering.

The question isn't "can we add AI?" It's "should we?" And if the answer is no, I say so. That's my job.

Most apps don't need AI. But when the problem actually calls for it — summarization, computer vision, classification, natural language — that's where the real work starts.

You can tell the difference with three questions:
→ Is there a non-AI solution that works reliably? If yes, use it.
→ Will the AI feature save real time or money, or is it just marketing?
→ Can we handle it when it's wrong? (And it will be wrong, sometimes.)

If those answers check out — build the AI. If not, fix your product first.

## 📅 DAY 10 — Wed Sep 2
**Pillar:** Productivity & Discipline (PROD)
**Style:** Life Coaching (COACH)
**Hashtags:** #Productivity #SoftwareEngineering #DeepWork

I used to brag about how many hours I coded in a day.

12-hour sessions. Red Bull and headphones. Skip lunch, skip the gym, code into the night.

I thought that was what serious engineers did. I was wrong.

The truth: I was writing maybe 3 good hours of code a day in those 12-hour sessions. The rest was scrolling, recompiling, context-switching, reading the same paragraph of documentation four times, pretending to work while I stared at a bug I was too tired to solve.

These days I code differently. I write code in 90-minute blocks. No phone. No Slack. Browser tabs closed except what I need. I take real breaks between blocks. I stop coding when my mind starts making stupid mistakes.

I ship more in 4-5 focused hours than I used to ship in 12.

Here's what I learned:
→ Your brain can do deep work for about 90 minutes. After that, you're pretending.
→ Two focused beats beat eight exhausted ones. Every single time.
→ The code you write at 11 PM is the code you debug at 10 AM tomorrow.
→ Walking away from a bug is often the fastest way to solve it. Your brain keeps working when you stop staring.

I'm not saying work less. I'm saying stop measuring your effort by the clock. Measure it by what actually ships.

Truth is — what time of day are you actually sharpest? Most people lie to themselves about this. 👇

The most unexpected skill that made me a better engineer?

Building Shopify themes.

Let me explain. Before I worked in e-commerce, I mostly built things from scratch. APIs, SPAs, DeFi apps, CV pipelines — the kind of work where you control the whole stack. You design the database. You write the backend. You build the frontend. Everything is your call.

Working across domains teaches you something that specializing too early hides: most engineering problems aren't about writing elegant code. They're about understanding a system you didn't build, finding where your work fits in it, and shipping within constraints that aren't yours to change.

That's the job. Not the blank-canvas greenfield work. The messy integration with systems that already exist, written by people you'll never meet, following conventions you didn't choose.

PropTech, FinTech, EdTech, E-commerce, DeFi, agricultural AI — every domain has its own rules. You learn faster by moving between them than by staying in one lane forever.

## 📅 DAY 12 — Fri Sep 4
**Pillar:** Career Growth (CAREER)
**Style:** Observational (OBSERVE)
**Hashtags:** #CareerGrowth #SoftwareEngineering #SeniorEngineer

I've worked with enough engineers now to tell you what separates the ones who keep growing from the ones who plateau.

It's not who knows the most frameworks. It's not who writes the fastest code. It's not even who's the smartest in the room.

It's one thing: how they respond when they're wrong.

I've seen junior engineers get defensive in code review and stall for six months. I've seen senior engineers say "you're right, my bad" and ship the fix in 20 minutes. The second group keeps getting promoted. The first group keeps wondering why.

Here's what I've watched the best engineers do when they're wrong:
→ They say so immediately. No deflection, no "well actually," no saving face.
→ They fix it. Fast.
→ They learn from it out loud — "I missed that, next time I'll check X first."
→ They don't make it personal. Bad code isn't a bad person.

If you can do those four things, you will outpace people who are "smarter" than you. Every single time.

Because engineering — at any level beyond solo projects — is collaborative. And nobody wants to work with someone whose ego is more expensive than their code.

Think about it. The best engineer you've ever worked with — I'll bet they're the easiest person to tell they're wrong.
